The Museum Companion

Project: The Museum Companion
Role: Product Designer
Duration: 5 weeks

Project Overview

I designed the Museum Planning Companion to help teachers handle field trip logistics and lesson planning in one unified tool. By organizing chaperone ratios, student tracking, and accessibility maps into a single dashboard, it handles the messy admin tasks so teachers can focus on guiding their students.

Research & Discovery

Before designing anything, I wanted to understand what actually makes museum field trips stressful for the people running them — not just assume. I conducted 5 interviews with elementary school teachers and reviewed 10 trip-planning forums to see where the friction really shows up.

What I Did

  • 5 remote interviews (30–40 min) with elementary educators who plan field trips at least once a year
  • Competitive review of 3 existing trip-planning tools/apps museums currently offer
  • Survey of 10 educators to quantify how much time is spent on logistics vs. lesson planning

What I Learned

  • Insight 1: "70% of teachers said calculating chaperone ratios was the single most time-consuming part of trip prep."
  • Insight 2: "Teachers with students needing accessibility accommodations had no single source of truth — they were piecing together info from emails, PDFs, and phone calls."
  • Insight 3: "Most planning happens on a phone between classes, not at a desk — so a desktop-only tool was a non-starter."

This research is where Tara — my primary persona — came from.

The Problem

Teachers like Tara struggle to keep track of chaperone limits and student safety rules on field trips. Because they don't have a simple, unified tool to calculate supervision ratios or see clear accessibility routes, planning layout routes and group safety ends up being a stressful game of guesswork.

Competitive Analysis

To understand where existing tools fall short for educators like Tara, I audited two direct competitors — the Royal Ontario Museum (ROM) and the Art Gallery of Ontario (AGO) — alongside one indirect competitor, Tiqets, a mobile-first ticketing platform.

ROM and AGO offer strong institutional content and are trusted cultural authorities, but both rely on legacy booking flows that push group organizers into manual phone or email coordination, and treat accessibility as a static PDF rather than an interactive feature. Tiqets, by contrast, delivers a fast, mobile-optimized checkout experience — but it's built for individual travelers, with no support for group-specific needs like chaperone ratios or curriculum planning.

Key Differentiators

  • Museum sites have authority but rely on slow, manual email booking
  • Ticketing apps are fast but designed for individuals, not school groups
  • Accessibility info is hidden in static PDFs rather than being built into the trip plan

This study revealed two clear opportunities: first, no existing platform connects the museum's educational content with the actual logistics of running a group trip; second, accessibility information is currently buried in static PDFs rather than being built directly into the planning tools.

This shaped my goal: build a planning companion that pairs educational content with real-time, accessibility-focused logistics, making the planning process much faster and more reassuring for educators.

Ideation

With the research insights in hand, I sketched out a few different directions before landing on the storyboard you'll see next.

Rejected

Direction A: Standalone Ratio Calculator

A simple tool focused exclusively on solving the chaperone-to-student math.

Trade-off: Solved one problem but left accessibility planning as a separate, disconnected step.

Rejected

Direction B: Chat-Based Planning Assistant

An interactive chat interface to guide teachers through booking steps.

Trade-off: Felt tedious for busy teachers who wanted to scan all details at a glance rather than chat.

Selected

Direction C: Unified Trip Dashboard

A single overview hub displaying trip status, ratio calculations, and accessibility options together.

Why Chosen: Combined ratio math and accessibility planning into one place, matching how teachers actually think about a trip: as one task, not three.

Usability Testing

I tested the prototype against a real scenario: log in, check an upcoming trip's approval status, calculate the chaperone ratio for 25 students, and export an accessible route map.

Insight 1: Educators could find what they needed, fast

During testing, all participants successfully identified the status of their upcoming trip within 2.5 seconds of landing on the dashboard. In my first layout, historical and upcoming field trips were clumped together, causing teachers to click past events by mistake. I fixed this by building an automated sorting filter that pins the current week's trip to the very top of the screen, which dropped user identification time down to a fraction of a second.

Insight 2: Teachers wanted a faster way to act on the data

Educators pointed out that keeping track of returned student slips was the most critical daily task. During testing, they requested a way to act on this data directly from the card state. Based on this, I added a "Send Reminder Ping" micro-action toggle directly inside the pending trip card to help teachers follow up with parents and close the administrative loop faster.

Insight 3: Pre-filled defaults prevented calculation errors

By building standard school board ratios directly into the form inputs as automatic defaults, the "Dynamic Defaults" system reduced ratio calculation errors to zero. School boards enforce strict legal chaperone-to-student ratios, but teachers often waste time hunting through PDF handbooks to find the rules. Pre-filling these guidelines automatically when a user enters their student count prevents ratio compliance rejections entirely before final submission.

At this stage, I moved from wireframes to high-fidelity layouts, testing key mobile interactions like thumb-friendly bottom menus and clear header sizing. I also made sure the ratio inputs flag validation errors immediately so teachers can't submit an incomplete plan, and built accessible path toggles directly into the map screen.

Design System

I built a simple design system for the app around a calm, natural matcha green and dark charcoal for high text contrast. This includes standard typography sizes, inputs, and reusable card containers to keep the interface consistent across all pages.

Inclusive Design & Accessibility

To make sure the app works for every teacher, I focused on accessibility at every step:

Features: I built wheelchair-accessible route toggles directly into the map screen and added accommodations tracking to the logistics form. This makes accessibility a core part of the setup rather than an afterthought.

Visuals: I used a clean, high-contrast palette (dark green on off-white) to meet text contrast standards, and kept the layout card-based so teachers can easily scan details on the move without feeling overwhelmed.

Takeaways & Next Steps

Working on Museum Companion pushed me to think about accessibility as a default, not an add-on — building the "Dynamic Defaults" system meant accessibility needs had to be part of the data model from day one, not bolted on after.

I was surprised by how much teachers appreciated moving the chaperone policy math directly into the form inputs. If I could start over, I’d tell myself to focus earlier on these small administrative frictions—they make a much bigger difference to a teacher's peace of mind than any visual detail.

Next Steps

  • Broad Usability Validation: Test the dashboard with a larger group of educators (10+) to see if the chaperone-ratio flow holds up across different school sizes.
  • Co-chaperone Interface: Explore a lightweight version for co-chaperones/parent volunteers, who currently have no view into the plan at all.
